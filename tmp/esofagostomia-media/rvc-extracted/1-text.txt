ABSTRACT 
Nasoesophageal and nasogastric feeding tubes are commonly used in dogs and cats, but inadvertent tracheal placement can 
cause serious complications. Although radiographs are widely used for confirming tube position, standardized interpretive 
guidelines are lacking. This study aimed to introduce and validate novel radiographic guidelines for improving tube position 
identification. Six blinded interpreters (one radiology intern, two emergency and critical care residents, one general practitioner, 
and two fourth-year veterinary students) with varying clinical experience assessed tube positioning on 256 lateral canine and 
feline cervical/thoracic radiographs before and after receiving the guidelines. True tube position was determined by clinical 
history and radiographic assessment by two board-certified radiologists. The proposed guidelines for identifying correctly 
positioned tubes included (1) incomplete superposition of the tube on the tracheal lumen, (2) dorsal tube position relative 
to the carina, and (3) relative to the larynx when included. The study included 129 esophageal-positioned (correct) and 127 
tracheal-positioned (incorrect) radiographs with nasoesophageal/nasogastric tubes. The implementation of the radiographic 
guidelines significantly improved the correct identification of tube positioning, increasing from 82.1% pre-guidelines to 95.8% 
post-guidelines ( p < 0.001). Additionally, “uncertain” assessments decreased from 14.1% pre-guidelines to 2.4% post-guidelines 
( p < 0.001). Before guidelines, interpreters correctly identified tube position in 89.1% of radiographs that included the larynx. 
However, guideline implementation did not significantly impact the correct interpretation of tube position assessment when 
the larynx was included. These results validate the clinical utility of a novel radiographic guideline to improve the accuracy and 
confidence of identification of nasoesophageal/nasogastric tube position in dogs and cats.

1 Introduction 
Early enteral feeding is widely recommended in both veterinary 
and human medicine to reduce morbidity and mortality rates 
[ 1–4 ]. Nasoesophageal and nasogastric tubes are frequently used 
for short-term enteral nutrition in companion animals [ 5, 6 ]. 
However, inadvertent tracheal placement can result in severe 
complications, including aspiration pneumonia, pulmonary 
hemorrhage, and pneumothorax, which may be fatal if not 
promptly addressed [ 7–13 ]. 
Thoracic radiographs are commonly used for noninvasive assess- 
ment of feeding tube position [ 6, 14 ]. Radiographic confirmation 
is considered the standard practice in human medicine and 
is widely recommended in veterinary medicine [ 7, 13, 15–18 ]. 
Although general radiographic techniques in small animals are 
well-documented, specific guidelines addressing radiographic 
projection, collimation, and tube evaluation, including visualiza- 
tion of the larynx, are lacking in veterinary medicine [ 19, 20 ]. 
Standardized radiographic guidelines have improved veterinary 
diagnostic accuracy in various applications, including cardiac 
assessment, pulmonary disease diagnosis, and fracture evaluation 
[ 21–23 ]. Without such guidelines, interpretation can be inconsis- 
tent, particularly for less commonly performed procedures [ 24 ]. 
This diagnostic accuracy study aimed to introduce and validate 
novel radiographic guidelines for the accurate identification of 
nasoesophageal and nasogastric tube position in dogs and cats. 
We hypothesized that (1) the proposed radiographic guidelines 
would improve the accuracy of nasoesophageal and nasogastric 
tube position identification, and (2) interpreter accuracy would 
improve when the larynx was included in the radiographic 
assessment of nasoesophageal/nasogastric tube placement. 
2 Materials and Methods 
2.1 Patient Selection 
This multi-institutional diagnostic accuracy study was prospec- 
tively designed and executed, using retrospectively collected 
lateral cervical and thoracic radiographs from dogs and cats. It 
was approved by the Clinical Research Ethical Review Board at 
the Royal Veterinary College (URN: SR2024-0098R0905). Medical 
records from 10 private and academic institutions, including 
the Queen Mother Hospital for Animals, were reviewed to 
identify dogs and cats that underwent radiographic assessment of 
nasoesophageal/nasogastric tube placements. Key words used for 
the search included “nasoesophageal tube,” “nasogastric tube,”
“tube check,” and “tube misplacement.” Dogs and cats were 
included if they underwent a digital lateral thoracic radiographic 
projection to assess the tube position. Radiographs were excluded 
if they were nondiagnostic due to suboptimal quality or body posi- 
tioning, or if the tube positioning was unclear. Final inclusion or 
exclusion decisions were made by two board-certified veterinary 
radiologists (E.F., European College of Veterinary Diagnostic 
Imaging (ECVDI), and F.V.G., ECVDI and American College of 
Veterinary Radiology) and a first-year ECVDI resident (A.V.C.). 
2.2 Data Recording and Radiographic Image 
Analysis 
Patient species was recorded. Dogs belonged to a wide variety 
of breeds, ranging from toy breeds to giant breeds, and cats 
were predominantly domestic shorthair. Individual body 
weights and breed documentation were not systematically 
recorded. Radiographic studies were anonymized by removing 
all identifying patient information. True tube position was 
determined on the basis of clinical history and radiographic 
reports from a board-certified veterinary radiologist. If the tube’s 
positioning remained unclear, the images were reviewed by two 
board-certified radiologists (E.F. and F.V.G.) to reach a consensus. 
If consensus could not be achieved, the radiograph was excluded. 
A power analysis determined that a minimum of 254 
radiographs was required to achieve 80% power in detecting a 
significant improvement ( p < 0.05) in accuracy following the 
implementation of the proposed guidelines. No explicit search 
timeframe for the radiographs was predefined, and instead, cases 
were collected in reverse chronological order (January 2024 
backwards) across the participating institutions, until the target 
sample size determined by the pretrial power calculation was 
reached (target: 254, included: 256). 
The lateral thoracic radiographs of dogs and cats with 
nasoesophageal and nasogastric tubes were compiled into a 
PowerPoint file (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA, 
2019) and randomized using a custom-coded method ( Supporting 
Information section) within PowerPoint to ensure a balanced 
distribution of intratracheal and intraesophageal tube images. Six 
blinded interpreters of different ranges of experience, including 
a radiology intern (S.G.R.), two American College of Veterinary 
Emergency and Critical Care (ACVECC) residents (H.M. and 
F.P.), two fourth-year veterinary students (V.M. and R.D.), and 
a general practice veterinary surgeon (J.B.), independently 
assessed tube positioning of a single lateral cervical and thoracic 
radiograph on a computer workstation (Apple Mac Pro, Apple, 
USA) equipped with a calibrated LCD flat screen monitor. Each 
interpreter reviewed radiographs twice, with a minimum 7-day 
interval between assessments; however, most interpreters had an 
interval of 3–4 weeks due to clinical commitments. 
For the second assessment, interpreters used the novel 
radiographic guidelines developed by two of the authors 
(E.F. and A.V.C.). The original PowerPoint was not rerandomized 
for this phase of the study. At each assessment, interpreters 
classified tube position as esophageal, tracheal, or uncertain. 
2.3 Proposed Radiographic Guidelines for 
Nasoesophageal and Nasogastric Tube Position 
The proposed guidelines provide a systematic, rapid, and practical 
approach for veterinarians to confirm correct esophageal tube 
placement and avoid misinterpretation. 
(1) Obtain a single lateral cervical and thoracic radiograph, 
ensuring inclusion of the cervical region, particularly the larynx.

to the carina, confirming its esophageal placement.

2.4 Statistical Analyses 
Data were checked and cleaned using Excel (Microsoft 
Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA, 2019). Statistical analyses 
were performed using Stata (version 16; Stata Corp LLC, College 
Station, TX, USA) by an Associate Professor in Companion Ani- 
mal Epidemiology with advanced training in statistics (D.O.N.). 
Chi-squared tests were used to assess associations between 
guideline provision and tube positioning accuracy, as well as the 
effect of laryngeal visualization on accuracy. These assessments 
were conducted separately before and after the provision of 
guidelines. Statistical significance was set at the 5% level. Inter- 
rater agreement using the Fleiss Kappa statistic between the six 
raters for each of the 256 lateral cervical and thoracic radiographs 
with three tube position options (esophagus, trachea, uncertain) 
was calculated separately for tube positioning opinions before 
and after provision of radiographic guidelines [ 25 ]. The Fleiss 
Kappa results were interpreted as follows: 1.00–0.81 almost 
perfect agreement, 0.80–0.61 substantial agreement, 0.60–0.4 
moderate agreement, 0.40–0.21 fair agreement, 0.20–0.00 slight 
agreement, and ≤ 0 poor agreement or no agreement [ 26 ]. 
3 Results 
3.1 Population 
The study included 256 lateral cervical and thoracic radiographs 
of dogs (186/256, 72.7%) and cats (70/256, 27.3%). Of these, 129/256 
(50.4%) radiographs showed tubes positioned in the esophagus 
(dogs 76/129, 58.9%; cats 53/129, 41.1%), whereas 127/256 (49.6%) 
showed tubes positioned in the trachea (dogs 110/127, 86.6%; 
cats 17/127, 13.4%). The larynx was included in 102/256 (39.8%) 
radiographs (dogs 73/186, 39.2%; cats 29/70, 41.4%). In most cases 
(239/256, 93.3%), the tube reached the level of the carina.

3.3 Inter-Rater Agreement Before and After 
Guidelines 
Inter-rater agreement between the six raters before provision of 
radiographic guidelines was 0.59 (Fleiss Kappa statistic), showing 
moderate agreement. The provision of radiographic guidelines 
significantly improved the inter-rater agreement between the 
six raters to 0.86 (Fleiss Kappa statistic), showing almost perfect 
agreement. 
3.4 Radiographic Assessment With and Without 
Laryngeal Visualization 
Before the provision of the radiographic guidelines, tube position 
was correctly identified in significantly fewer images without 
laryngeal inclusion (716/924, 77.5%) than in those including the 
larynx (545/612, 89.1%) ( p < 0.001). After the implementation of 
the guidelines, no significant difference was observed for correct 
interpretation rates between radiographs excluding (881/924, 
95.4%) and including the larynx (590/612, 96.4%) ( p = 0.452). An 
example of a case that generated mixed interpretations before 
guideline provision and in which the larynx is not included is 
presented in Figure 5 .

4 Discussion 
This is the first study to propose radiographic guidelines to 
improve the accuracy and confidence in identifying nasoe- 
sophageal tube positioning in dogs and cats. Various methods 
have been described in both human and veterinary literature 
for determining nasoesophageal tube position, including pH 
testing, capnography, and assessing for negative pressure at 
the thoracic inlet during placement [ 17, 18, 27 ]. However, 
radiography remains the standard of care method for confirming 
appropriate nasoesophageal and nasogastric tube position, as 
the other aforementioned alternative tests have limited evidence 
supporting their reliability [ 7, 28 ]. The proposed novel three-point 
radiographic guidelines are designed to be rapid, straightforward, 
and reliable, enhancing their clinical applicability and utility 
for both radiologist and non-radiologist veterinary professionals 
across emergency, critical care, and general practice settings. 
The findings of this study support the first hypothesis as the 
novel radiographic guidelines significantly increased the correct 
identification of nasoesophageal/nasogastric tube position. The 
guidelines enhanced the identification of tube position in both the 
esophagus and trachea. For esophageal tube placements, correct 
interpretation increased from 88.8% to 98.7% ( p < 0.001), with 
“uncertain”assessmentsdecreased from 8.9% to 0.8% ( p < 0.001). 
Similarly, for tracheal tube placements, correct interpretation 
improved from 75.3% to 92.8% ( p < 0.001), with “uncertain”
assessments decreasing from 19.4% to 4.1% ( p < 0.001). A notable 
finding was the high level of uncertainty among interpreters 
when evaluating tubes positioned in the trachea. Before the intro- 
duction of guidelines, tracheal-positioned tubes were correctly 
identified in a high percentage of cases (75.3%), yet 19.4% of assess- 
ments were categorized as “uncertain.”Misplaced tracheal tube 
lies entirely within, and superimposes on, the tracheal lumen, an 
appearance interpreters often recognize as abnormal but cannot 
confidently classify without explicit criteria. Esophageal tube 
positioning more commonly shows visible separation or dorsal 
deviation. The guidelines, especially the “no complete overlap”
and “dorsal to carina” criteria, provided objective landmarks 
that resolved this ambiguity (uncertain responses for tracheal 
tubes fell to 4.1%). Additionally, this likely reflects appropriate 
clinical caution among the interpreters, who represented a range 
of veterinary trainees and practitioners rather than specialist 
radiologists, given the potentially life-threatening consequences 
of mistaking a tracheal for an esophageal placement. The 
implementation of the guidelines has provided clearer criteria, 
reduced uncertainty, and improved diagnostic confidence. 
Overall, the correct interpretations of tube positioning 
significantly increased, from 82.1% before guidelines to 95.8% 
after guidelines ( p < 0.001), and the percentage of “uncertain”
assessments decreased, from 14.1% before guidelines to 2.4% after 
guidelines ( p < 0.001). These results indicate that interpreters 
reported significantly greater confidence in their evaluations 
following the application of the guidelines, which enhanced 
diagnostic accuracy. To ensure the reliability of these findings, 
the study implemented a comprehensive validation process 
involving multiple interpreters and a large dataset of radiographs

The second study hypothesis, that interpreter accuracy improves 
when the larynx is included in the radiographic assessment 
of nasoesophageal/nasogastric tube placement, was strongly 
supported in pre-guidelines assessment. Before guidelines 
implementation, interpreters correctly identified tube position 
in 89.1% of radiographs that included the larynx, whereas after 
guidelines introduction, accuracy increased to 96.4%, though this 
improvement was not statistically significant, suggesting that 
laryngeal inclusion already provided a high level of accuracy, 
reflecting the benefit of seeing the cranial portion of the tube 
course. The authors propose that post-guideline improvements 
were largely attributed to two key points: [ 1 ] The tube should 
not completely overlap with the tracheal lumen, and [ 2 ] the 
tube should be positioned dorsally to the carina. In most cases 
(239/256, 93.3%), the tube reached the level of the carina. 
However, this is not always the case. In such cases, the carina 
criterion cannot be applied, and inclusion of the larynx becomes 
the decisive anatomic landmark. Additionally, the authors do 
not suggest further advancement of the tube, given the risk of 
tracheobronchial perforation, which can result in pulmonary 
hemorrhage and/or pneumothorax [ 8, 13 ]. Therefore, including 
the larynx in the radiographic assessment is recommended as 
an additional confirmation point for improved accuracy in the 
evaluation of tube positioning. Radiographically, the larynx is 
easier to delineate in dogs than in cats, mainly due to size differ- 
ences and the presence of laryngeal mineralization in older dogs, 
chondrodystrophic breeds, and large-breed dogs. The cricoid 
cartilage is often the most radiographically distinct structure [ 19 ]. 
Species significantly affected diagnostic confidence and accuracy.

(97.86%) and markedly fewer uncertain responses (1.90%) than 
dogs (94.98% and 2.60%, respectively; p = 0.012). This finding 
initially appears counterintuitive because feline patients are 
generally smaller and have a narrower mediastinum with 
less fat separating structures, leading to superposition and 
reduced contrast on radiographs [ 29, 30 ]. However, tracheal

The current study had several limitations. The effect of interpreter 
experience on the accuracy of nasoesophageal tube interpretation 
was not assessed due to the small number of interpreters and 
the variability in their levels of experience. A formal subgroup 
analysis stratified by level of expertise was not performed, 
as the study was not powered for this comparison, and the 
limited number of observers per category would have resulted 
in low statistical power. Future studies with larger numbers 
of interpreters across defined experience categories would 
be valuable to quantify how much the guidelines reduce 
interobserver variability and benefit less experienced clinicians 
in particular. There was also a potential for temporal bias, 
as interpreters may have gained experience when reviewing 
radiographs for the second time following the introduction of 
the guidelines. Although the minimum wash-out period was 7 
days, most interpreters had intervals of 3–4 weeks due to clinical 
scheduling. Although a longer fixed interval would have been 
ideal, this was constrained by interpreter availability across 
multiple institutions. However, the substantial improvement in 
accuracy and the marked reduction in “uncertain” responses 
after guideline provision strongly suggest that the observed 
effect is primarily attributable to the guidelines rather than 
simple familiarization with the images. The study prospectively 
aimed for a roughly equal proportion of nasoesophageal tubes 
positioned in the trachea and esophagus to ensure a robust 
comparison between intended and unintended placements. 
However, this ratio may not reflect the real-world clinical 
scenarios. Given the life-threatening implications of tracheal 
misplacement, the inclusion of a substantial number of tracheal 
tube cases was considered necessary. Although the relative 
contribution of each individual criterion was not quantitatively 
assessed, the synergistic effect of the three-point checklist is 
evidenced by the dramatic reduction in both misinterpretations 
and diagnostic uncertainty. A further limitation was the use of a 
single lateral radiograph from each animal rather than multiple 
orthogonal projections. However, the high levels of correct 
identification of position post-guidelines suggest that a single 
lateral radiograph is sufficient for an accurate interpretation, sup- 
porting the feasibility of adopting the novel guidelines in general 
practice, where obtaining multiple projections may not always 
be practical [ 31 ]. Individual body weights were not available, 
precluding formal analysis of patient size independent of species. 
Nonetheless, our dataset recorded the majority of the dog breeds, 
ranging from toy to giant breeds based on clinical records and 
radiographic appearance, as well as a feline population, providing 
a wide range of patient conformations. This diversity supports 
the robustness of the guidelines across markedly different patient 
sizes. Additionally, the exact nasoesophageal/nasogastric tube 
type and diameter were not recorded. However, all the tubes were 
sufficiently radiopaque with a similar radiographic appearance 
(typically two parallel radiopaque lines) for inclusion. The 
consistent radiographic performance of the guidelines across 
this heterogeneous tube population supports their applicability 
regardless of specific tube material or size.

Finally, the multicenter nature of the study meant that 
radiographs were obtained with different machines and in 
varying clinical settings. However, the authors believe that rather 
than being a limitation, this diversity strengthens the study’s 
applicability across a broad range of veterinary practices. 
In conclusion, this is the first study to establish radiographic 
guidelines for the accurate identification of nasoesophageal 
and nasogastric tube positions in dogs and cats. The results 
demonstrate a strong clinical benefit from these novel guidelines, 
improving the accuracy of tube localization and increasing 
interpreter confidence. Consequently, these guidelines are 
proposed as a valuable new tool in veterinary practice to 
enhance patient safety and the overall quality of radiographic 
evaluations.
