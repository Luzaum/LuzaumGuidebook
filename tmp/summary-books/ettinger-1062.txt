OVERVIEW: THE 
ANAPLASMATACEAE AND 
RICKETTSIACEAE FAMILIES
T
he primary causes of canine ehrlichio-
sis are the intracellular Gram-negative 
bacteria Ehrlichia canis, Ehrlichia ewin-
gii, and Ehrlichia chaeensis, belonging to the 
family Anaplasmataceae. Other bacteria within 
this family are Anaplasma platys and Ana-
plasma phagocytophilum, which cause canine 
thrombocytic and granulocytic anaplasmosis, 
respectively, and Neorickettsia helminthoeca,
the agent of salmon poisoning disease (SPD). 
Rickettsia rickettsii, the cause of Rocky Moun-
tain spotted fever (RMSF), and other spotted 
fever group (SFG) rickettsiae belong to the 
family Rickettsiaceae. e families Rickettsia-
ceae and Anaplasmataceae are related through 
the order Rickettsiales (Table 193.1).
ese organisms are transmitted to dogs 
and cats by arthropod or trematode vec-
tors (Table 193.2), are maintained in nature 
through infection of wildlife reservoir hosts, and can be transmitted 
by blood transfusion. eir geographic distribution is restricted 
to that of their vectors and intermediate hosts. Several of these 
pathogens cause human disease. Dogs, therefore, serve as potential 
sentinels for human infection and precautions should be taken to 
prevent transmission while handling engorged ticks, blood, and tis-
sue from dogs with suspected infections. Because of shared arthro-
pod vectors and/or concurrent exposure to multiple vector ticks, 
coinfections with more than one of these pathogens (as well as 
other vector-borne pathogens) can occur and may complicate the 
clinical picture. Severity of clinical signs depends on the size of the 
inoculum, host immunity, and pathogen strain virulence.
EHRLICHIOSIS
EHRLICHIA CANIS
Etiology and Epidemiology
Ehrlichia canis causes canine monocytic ehrlichiosis (CME), a disease 
of dogs exposed to ticks worldwide. e organism infects circulating 
monocytes and, within its cytoplasm, forms morulae (Latin for “mul-
berry”; a cluster of bacteria). Ehrlichia canis is transmitted by Rhipi-
cephalus sanguineus ticks. Infection has been reported in dogs from 
Asia, Africa, Europe, and the Americas (ch. 9). In the United States 
(USA), it is diagnosed most frequently in southern states. However,

Clinical Signs
Acute Phase The course of CME is divided into acute and 
chronic phases, although discrete phases are not always clinically 
distinguishable. Transmission can occur within hours of tick attach-
ment and acute signs of disease occur 8 to 20 days later. Common 
vague signs include lethargy (ch. 10), fever (ch. 16), inappetence 
(ch. 17), and weight loss (ch. 18). Replication of the organism in 
reticuloendothelial tissues is associated with generalized lymphad-
enomegaly and splenomegaly (ch. 182). Ocular and nasal discharge 
(ch. 35), peripheral edema (ch. 30), and less commonly, petechial 
and ecchymotic hemorrhages (ch. 29) are observed. Neurologic 
signs, likely caused by meningeal inflammation or hemorrhage, 
include twitching, ataxia, seizures, vestibular signs, hyperesthesia, 
and cranial nerve deficits (ch. 242). Thrombocytopenia (ch. 59) 
and sometimes mild leukopenia (ch. 58) and anemia (ch. 57) occur 
1 to 4 weeks after infection (ch. 176). Transient proteinuria, which 
resolves within 6 weeks of infection, has been reported.1 2 Dogs may 
recover from the acute phase after 2 to 4 weeks without treatment.
Subclinical Phase Aer the acute phase, some dogs remain 
subclinically infected for months to years. ey may have mild 
persistent thrombocytopenia. Organisms may sequester within 
the spleen and evade host immune systems through antigenic 
variation.3
Chronic Phase “Chronic ehrlichiosis” ranges in severity 
from mild to life-threatening, with signs that include leth-
argy, inappetence, bleeding tendencies, pallor, fever, weight 
loss, lymphadenomegaly, splenomegaly, anterior uveitis (ch. 
13), retinal hemorrhage, retinal detachment, polyuria/poly-
dipsia (PU/PD, ch. 53), and edema (Figure 193.1). Bleeding 
tendencies are the result of thrombocytopenia and platelet 
dysfunction.4 Cutaneous and mucosal petechial or ecchymotic 
hemorrhages, epistaxis, melena, hematochezia, hematuria, 
and prolonged bleeding from venipuncture sites have been 
reported (ch. 171). Polymyositis may develop, with muscle 
atrophy and tetraparesis (ch. 23).5 Secondary opportunistic 
infections, including viral papillomatosis, protozoal infections, 
and bacteriuria, also occur, although mechanisms of immune 
suppression and persistence of E. canis have not yet been elu-
cidated (Figure 193.2).6
Pancytopenia on a complete blood count (CBC) caused by 
hypoplasia of all bone marrow cells typifies the severe chronic 
form of ehrlichiosis (ch. 176). Thrombocytopenia and nonre-
generative anemia are common. Moderate to marked granular 
lymphocytosis (≤17,000/mcL) and bone marrow plasmacytosis 
may occur. Some dogs have a monoclonal gammopathy, similar 
to that seen with lymphocytic leukemia or multiple myeloma 
(ch. 60). Dogs with well-differentiated lymphocytosis or an 
otherwise unexplained monoclonal gammopathy should be 
tested for E. canis infection.7 Serum chemistry abnormalities 
include hypoalbuminemia, hyperglobulinemia, and increased 
serum alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and alkaline phos-
phatase (ALP) activities. The hyperglobulinemia is usually a 
polyclonal gammopathy. A protein-losing nephropathy may 
develop as a result of an immune-complex glomerulonephritis, 
possibly associated with azotemia (ch. 302). Dogs with central 
nervous system (CNS) involvement may have increased cere-
brospinal fluid (CSF) protein concentrations and cell counts8
(ch. 109).
