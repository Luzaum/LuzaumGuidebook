Treatment and Outcome
Long-term treatment for animals with MG consists of treatment 
via AChE inhibitors, as well as immunosuppressive therapy to 
reduce autoantibody production against the AChR. Anticholin-
esterase drugs such as pyridostigmine bromide (oral) or neostig-
mine (intramuscular) are the mainstays of supportive therapy and 
they work by inhibiting AChE so that ACh remains within the 
synaptic cle longer.5 ese medications must be carefully titrated 
to eect, and neostigmine is generally used only in hospitalized 
animals in acute crisis or with profound regurgitation. Both med-
ications have a narrow therapeutic window, and signicant side-
eects such as bradycardia, hypersalivation, vomiting, diarrhea, 
muscle cramping, and weakness can occur. Clinical signs associ-
ated with excessive anticholinergic drug administration may be 
dicult to distinguish from those of uncontrolled MG and careful 
monitoring is required at the time of therapy initiation.
Immunosuppressive agents such as cyclosporine, mycopheno-
late mofetil (MMF), and azathioprine (chs. 144 and 169) are fre-
quently used in the treatment of MG to reduce levels of circulating 
autoantibodies.44 45 Extrapolating from the human literature, MMF 
was once considered the drug of choice for canine MG; however, 
a recent study comparing dogs treated with MMF and pyridostig-
mine to those treated with pyridostigmine alone showed no dier-
ence in survival time or remission rate between groups.45
Corticosteroids can also be eective in the treatment of MG, 
but should be used with caution in animals with aspiration pneu-
monia (ch. 227).5 Corticosteroids may potentiate muscle weak-
ness and exacerbate clinical signs in some patients, and a gradual 
increase in corticosteroid dose is recommended to help avoid this 
complication. Other strategies for immunomodulation may also 
be employed in patients with MG, including therapeutic plasma 
exchange and intravenous administration of immune globulin 
(IVIG).46 47 Neither technique has been thoroughly evaluated 
for MG in a controlled veterinary study, although their utility 
is supported by small case series and ecacy in other immune-
mediated diseases. A long-term spontaneous remission rate as 
high as 88% has been reported for dogs with MG; spontaneous 
remission is not a characteristic of feline MG.32 39
CONGENITAL MYASTHENIC SYNDROME
CMS has been reported in a number of pure and mixed-breed dogs, 
and cats.30 A deciency of the AChR is assumed in most breeds and 
has been documented in Jack Russell Terriers, Springer Spaniels, and 
Miniature Dachshunds.30 48 49 Abnormalities in ACh synthesis or 
in AChE function have also been reported. CMS oen aects mul-
tiple animals in the same litter and clinical signs are usually apparent 
beginning around 6-9 weeks of age. Generalized limb and cervical 
muscle weakness that worsen with activity are common. Megaesoph-
agus is variably present. An autosomal recessive-mode of inheritance 
has been demonstrated in Smooth Fox Terriers, Jack Russell Terriers, 
and Gammel Dansk Honsehund (GDH) dogs.50 51
Measurement of AChR autoantibodies is not useful in diagnosis 
of CMS. A denitive diagnosis is made in some cases by quanti-
cation of AChR in a muscle biopsy. Treatment with pyridostigmine 
oen results in improvement; however, long-term response to anti-
cholinesterase therapy is variable and many reports describe death or 
euthanasia of aected animals due to diculty in controlling clinical 
signs. Exceptions to this are CMS in Miniature Dachshunds, where 
signs spontaneously resolved by 6 months of age, and GDHs, which 
tend to remain stable with mild clinical signs.48 51
OTHER
Although not a disease of the NMJ, exercise-induced collapse (EIC) 
of Labrador Retrievers causes fatigue and accid para- or tetraparesis 
during or aer strenuous activity. Examination ndings are normal 
at rest. Ataxia, pelvic limb weakness, and collapse develop in asso-
ciation with intense or prolonged eld training, hunting, or vigorous 
play (Video 253.1). Clinical signs may progress to involve the thoracic 
limbs. Recovery occurs with rest, and most dogs are clinically normal 
within 30 minutes; however, severe episodes can result in death.52-54
e condition is heritable in Labradors, associated with an 
autosomal recessively inherited mutation (Arg256Leu) in the gene 
encoding for the protein dynamin 1 (DNM1). DNM1 is expressed 
at synaptic terminal membranes within the CNS and is important 
in the recycling of synaptic vesicles during high frequency neuro-
logical stimulation.52 55 A collapse phenotype is also recognized 
in Labradors and other breeds in the absence of homozygosity for 
the DNM-1 mutation.55
Dogs with DNM1-associated EIC (d-EIC) typically experi-
ence their rst episode of collapse before 2 years of age.54 Loss 
of the patellar reex is common during an episode, and dogs 
maintain normal mental status.54 56 Hyperthermia and elevated 
plasma lactate oen develop during an episode, but are not dis-
tinct from what is observed in normal exercising dogs.56 Serum 
lactate/pyruvate ratios are normal in dogs with EIC both at rest 
and during exercise, and muscle biopsies are unremarkable.56 A 
genetic test for the Arg256Leu DNM1 mutation is now commer-
cially available to assist in diagnosis. While there is no cure for 
EIC, aected dogs have an excellent prognosis if they refrain from 
intense physical activity known to trigger episodes.56

central nervous system to synapse with the postganglionic nerve 
in ganglia that are either close to (parasympathetic) or distant 
from (sympathetic) the target organ.1 2
