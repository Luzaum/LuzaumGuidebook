INTRODUCTION
F
eline 
idiopathic/interstitial 
cysti-
tis (FIC) is the most common cause 
of chronic lower urinary tract signs 
(cLUTS), which includes variable combina-
tions of straining, hematuria, pollakiuria, 
and periuria, in domestic cats.1 Cats show-
ing these signs have been referred to as hav-
ing “feline idiopathic/interstitial cystitis” 
only because of their similarities to humans 
with interstitial cystitis (IC; now sometimes 
referred to as bladder pain syndrome/inter-
stitial cystitis). IC is characterized clinically 
by dicult, painful, and frequent urinations 
without a diagnosable cause, and oen a 
variety of comorbid health problems.2
FIC was once classied as a “feline lower 
urinary tract disorder” (FLUTD)3 because 
cats present with cLUTS. FIC does not seem 
to originate in the bladder, however. Just 
as jaundice can present as a dermatologic 
or ophthalmic sign, it is neither a dermatologic nor ophthal-
mic disorder. Research has identied genetic factors, epigenetic 
eects, and environmental inuences associated with risk for 
(F)IC, documented that comorbid disorders oen occur before 
the onset of the lower urinary tract signs (LUTS), and demon-
strated how much systemic involvement can be present in these 
patients.1 One of us (CATB) has proposed the term “Pandora syn-
drome” to describe the multiple health problems present in some 
of these cats (as in people with chronic overlapping pain condi-
tions), because it does not identify any specic cause or organ, 
and tries to capture the dismay and dispute associated with the 
identication of so many problems outside the organ of interest of 
any particular subspecialty.4
Regardless of the name eventually applied to this syndrome, 
we urge clinicians and investigators to look beyond the bladder 
when obtaining a history, performing physical examinations, and 
considering pertinent diagnostics and treatments to manage the 
clinical signs of their patients. To avoid confusion, we will refer 
to this syndrome here as FIC, because it is the term used in most 
published studies.
We currently perceive FIC to describe abnormalities result-
ing from persistent activation of the central threat response sys-
tem (CTRS).1 Persistent activation of the CTRS can occur when 
chronic perception of threat exceeds the cat’s perception of con-
trol. is mobilizes persistent activity in variable combinations 
of the nervous (sensory, autonomic, and motor), endocrine, and 
immune systems, which can eventually also aect the cat’s behav-
ior, as well as bladder, skin, gastrointestinal tract, and other organ 
function.5
e variability among individual cats’ responses to chronic 
perception of threat may result from familial dierences in 
organ-specic vulnerability, and to the intensity of exposure to 
threatening events that can durably sensitize the CTRS to the 
environment. Suciently harsh events can sensitize the CTRS 
at any time of life, but such events oen occur early in life, even 
before birth, when the CTRS is most plastic and vulnerable to 
the events via stress hormone signals communicated to the fetus 
through the placenta by its mother.6 Our current understand-
ing of FIC is informed by knowledge gained from studies of cats 
and people with the naturally occurring disorder, and of induced 
models in rodents.
ETIOPATHOGENESIS OF FIC
RECENT STUDIES—CATS
A review of the pathophysiology and management of cats with 
cLUTS was published in 2019.1 More recently, Kaul etal.,7
reported on the recurrence rate and long-term course of 101 
cats that were presented for evaluation of cLUTS from 2010 to 
2013 based on telephone interviews using a questionnaire with 
cat owners. ey reported that 52 cats were diagnosed with FIC, 
21 with urolithiasis, and 13 with bacterial urinary tract infection 
(UTI); no denitive diagnosis was made for 15 cats. Of the 86 cats 
with a known diagnosis, the recurrence rate, dened as recurrence 
of clinical signs following a disease-free interval of at least 10 days 
aer the previous episode, was 58%. No signicant dierence in 
recurrence rate was identied between the 3 groups (FIC, uroli-
thiasis, or bacterial UTI). Twenty-one cats (42%) had 1 relapse, 
12 (24%) had 2 relapses, 10 (20%) had 3, and 7 cats (14%) had 4 
to 8 relapses within a median observation period of 38 months 
(range 0.5-138 months). Fourteen cats (28%) suered from dier-
ent causes of LUTS during dierent episodes. e mortality due 
to LUTS among all 101 cats was 5%.
Fourteen of the 50 cats (28%) with recurring LUTS episodes 
were diagnosed with dierent etiologies at dierent episodes; 
the most common combination of diagnoses in these cats was 
FIC and urolithiasis. Although limited by recall bias and a lack 
of statistical power, the study demonstrated that recurrent epi-
sodes of LUTS in aected cats can occur commonly, and cannot 
be assumed to result from the same etiology. It is worth recalling 
that Osborne etal., listed some 30 possible causes of LUTS, from 
anatomical anomalies to toxins, nearly 40 years ago.3
RECENT STUDIES—HUMANS AND OTHER ANIMALS
Humans
IC in people recently has been classied as a “chronic overlap-
ping pain condition” by the National Institutes of Health,8 as 
“nociplastic”9 and “chronic primary pain”10 by the International 
Association for the Study of Pain, and as “centralized pain” by 
the Multi-Disciplinary Approach to the Study of Chronic Pelvic 
Pain.11 All of these terms describe pain primarily originating 
from the patient’s central nervous system (CNS).11 is change 
from a “nociceptive” to a “nociplastic” perspective resulted from 
a paradigm shi in understanding of chronic pain, which is now

CNSs receive constant input from external “envirosensors” 
(auditory, gustatory, olfactory, somatosensory, and visual) and from 
internal sensors throughout the body.13 Brodal14 recently suggested 
the term homeoceptor rather than nociceptor to describe small sen-
sory bers based on their response patterns to a variety of environ-
mental stimuli, and because nociceptive activity is neither necessary 
nor sucient to evoke pain (although it is required to protect the 
body from injury15). Moreover, no innate properties of nociceptive 
bers endow them, and only them, with the capacity to evoke pain.16
Aer variable excitatory and inhibitory processing in the spi-
nal cord,16 information from the periphery is transmitted to the 
brain. Very early in life,17 animals begin to integrate these inputs 
with their contexts to manage subsequent interactions with the 
world. is integration permits the animal to develop some form 
of anticipation of sensory input. e resulting system has been 
described as “predictive processing (or coding)”18 and “interac-
tivist.”19 Regardless of the process, sensory information reaching 
the brain is integrated into a “decision” that forms the basis for 
subsequent action, whether or not it is consciously perceived. 
At its most basic, the CNS “decides” whether to continue with 
the present course of interaction with the world, or to change 
course. Depending on the salience of the message for the indi-
vidual’s action and motivation, threatening messages can change 
the course of behavior to withdrawal and avoidance.20
Acutely dangerous, threatening situations can activate ight, 
ght, or freeze reactions, which can suppress the pain of injuries 
in the service of survival.21 22 In contrast, persistent exposure, 
particularly to toxic levels of threat, can exacerbate rather than 
suppress chronic pain symptoms.23 If the animal “learns” that 
withdrawal and avoidance occur most eciently in the presence 
of signals from a particular source in the body, pain may be per-
ceived to arise from this source even when the threat is external 
to the body.4 us, pain is a decision a brain makes to protect 
animals from threat, whatever the source.23
Other Animals
FIC in cats has limitations as a model of IC in humans.24 Acquir-
ing aected cats depends on both veterinarian and owner cooper-
ation, and such cats are more expensive to maintain in laboratory 
animal facilities than are rodents, are outbred, and lack many of 
the molecular research tools available for rodents. For these rea-
sons, stress models of IC recently have been developed in rodents, 
including water avoidance25 and early life stressors,26 to investi-
gate eects of environmental threats on visceral function, noci-
ception, anxiety, and depression.27
Water-avoidance stress (WAS) has been used for investigating 
mechanisms of visceral pain associated with IC in adult rats.28-
30 Briey, WAS is induced by placing rats predisposed to anxiety 
on pedestals in the center of tanks lled with water at room tem-
perature for an hour a day for 10 consecutive days. is stressor 
induces some of the same histopathologic and clinical features 
of (F)IC, including urothelial disruption, angiogenesis, mast cell 
inltration, and increased urinary frequency, but not in the fre-
quency of non-voiding contractions during cystometry under 
anesthesia. Lower hind paw withdrawal thresholds and increased 
withdrawal responses to suprapubic stimulation, which persisted 
for more than a month, also have been reported.29
Adverse early life experiences appear to be more common in 
people31 and cats32 with (F)IC than in healthy controls. Adverse 
early life experience models, including maternal separation33
and limited nest materials,33 34 recently have been used for inves-
tigating eects of environmental threats on visceral function, 
nociception, anxiety, and depression. Aer reviewing the eects 
of exposure of healthy neonatal rodents to various stressors on 
chronic visceral pain, Greenwood-Van Meerveld and Johnson33
concluded that chronic stress early in life can result in gene × envi-
ronment interactions. Such interactions can induce epigenetically 
mediated changes in neuronal circuitry, leading to neuroplasticity 
and increased expression of pro-nociceptive neurotransmitters in 
the CNS of aected individuals. is mechanism also has been 
described for sensitization of the CTRS in patients with (F)IC.6
MANIFESTATIONS/SIGNS OF FIC IN THE HOME 
ENVIRONMENT
As mentioned, FIC typically presents with a variety of LUTS, 
including dysuria, stranguria, pollakiuria, hematuria and peri-
uria.35 Cat owners may not be familiar with the signs of painful 
urination or their cats’ normal urinary habits (e.g., frequency of uri-
nation, amount of urine typically produced by cats). Some owners 
may mistake FIC for spraying or marking behavior (ch. 11). Ques-
tions about the frequency, location, positioning of urination (e.g., 
vertical versus horizontal surface) can help rule out urine (spray) 
marking.36 Owners are oen motivated to present the cat for medi-
cal assessment when they observe blood in the urine or when the 
cat starts urinating outside the litter box. Other signs associated 
with stress, such as overgrooming (especially of the caudal ventral 
abdomen and inner thighs), hiding, and biting or scratching, may 
be overlooked.37 Because of FIC’s intermittent nature, with signs 
that wax and wane, owners may mistakenly believe that the prob-
lem has resolved, until another episode of FIC occurs, oen trig-
gered by stressful situations in the environment.36
DIAGNOSIS1
We take several factors into consideration, including the num-
ber of episodes the cat has had, the severity of the cat’s clinical 
signs (including comorbidities), and the nancial resources of 
the owner.1 No sensitive, specic, and clinically available test 
currently exists to diagnose FIC, so the diagnosis rests on signal-
ment; history, including inclusion of cat and known environmen-
tal risk factors; exclusion of other (common) causes of LUTS; and 
response to therapy (Figure 311.1).
RESPONSE TO MEMO
Most middle-aged cats with cLUTS are eventually diagnosed with 
FIC,38,39 so we oen begin by oering multimodal environmental 
modication (MEMO)40 for FIC and monitoring the cat’s response. 
We watch carefully for a positive response to support the diagno-
sis of FIC, particularly if the owner has nancial constraints and 
declines additional diagnostic testing (e.g., urinalysis, imaging).
ABDOMINAL RADIOGRAPHS
If the cat does not improve, or if clinical signs return within days 
to weeks despite implementation of appropriate MEMO, we rec-
ommend abdominal radiography because the next most common 
dierential in cats with cLUTS is urinary stone disease (ch. 309). 
Abdominal radiographs that include the entire urinary system 
from the cranial pole of the right kidney to beyond the end of the 
distal urethra help detect radiopaque stones; approximately 20% 
of cats with LUTS have bladder stones.7
