ANEMIAS DUE TO HEMOLYSIS
PATHOPHYSIOLOGY
H
emolytic anemias are caused by 
an accelerated erythrocyte (red 
blood cell [RBC]) destruction 
and decrease of the normal RBC life span 
(dog: 100-10 days, cat: 70-75 days) due 
to an intrinsic (e.g., erythroenzymopa-
thy) or extrinsic (e.g., antibody formation 
against the RBC membrane) defect. Hemo-
lytic anemias are usually regenerative, 
and extramedullary hematopoiesis in the 
spleen and liver may occur. Hemolysis can 
be extravascular or intravascular. Extra-
vascular hemolysis is the predominant 
form and refers to phagocytosis of RBCs by 
the mononuclear phagocytic system of the 
spleen, liver, and bone marrow. Damaged 
(as well as physiologically senescent) RBCs 
are rst sequestered, then phagocytosed, 
and nally degraded. Hemoglobin (Hb) 
is degraded to bilirubin, iron, and amino 
acids. Depending on the extent of hemoly-
sis, hyperbilirubinemia and bilirubinuria 
may result, although serum bilirubin 
may be normal in the case of slow chronic 
hemolytic anemia (especially in cats). Less 
commonly, RBCs are lysed in the blood 
vessels (intravascular hemolysis) as a result 
of membrane damage or cellular fragmen-
tation (resulting in hemoglobinemia and 
hemoglobinuria). Renal damage caused 
by Hb is very rare in small animals.1 e 
pathophysiological classification of hemolytic anemias is pre-
sented in Table 174.1
Clinical Presentation
Clinical signs depend on the severity and rate of hemolysis, com-
plications, and the underlying disease. Mild and chronic cases 
show no or only mild, nonspecic signs. Severe hemolytic anemias 
are associated with sudden weakness, collapse, and signs of shock. 
In addition to the typical signs of anemia, icterus (with plasma 
bilirubin concentration >1.5- mg/dL [>6-34 micromol/L]) and 
pigmenturia (hemoglobinuria or/and bilirubinuria), fever, hep-
ato- and splenomegaly, and tachypnea may be present.
Laboratory Findings
Usually, hemolytic anemias are regenerative (polychromasia, 
reticulocytosis [dog >60,000/mcL, cat >40,000/mcL], increased 
numbers of nucleated RBCs, macrocytosis), although they may be 
nonregenerative during the acute phase, i.e., approximately the rst 
3 days. Cats have  types of reticulocytes: punctate (-10 granules) 
and aggregated reticulocytes. e aggregated reticulocytes corre-
spond to canine reticulocytes (ch. 57). Aggregated reticulocytes 
become punctate reticulocytes aer about 1 hours, and the latter 
circulate over a period of -3 weeks and therefore do not allow 
any assessment regarding the current activity of erythropoiesis.
Punctate reticulocytes can also be found in healthy non-anemic 
cats. Aggregated reticulocytes represent polychromatophilic 
RBCs on blood smears. Hyperbilirubinemia may be present in all 
forms of hemolysis; hepatopathy or internal hemorrhage should 
be considered as dierential diagnoses. Plasma protein concentra-
tions are usually normal or elevated. e osmotic fragility of the 
RBCs is generally increased, regardless of the cause.3-6
