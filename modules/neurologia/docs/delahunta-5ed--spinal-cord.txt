Around that time, the owner accidentally stepped on the cat and over the subsequent 24 to 48 hours noticed an abnormal gait. Such an accident had never occurred before. The cat was brought to the referring veterinarian, who diagnosed a head tilt and placed the animal on corticosteroid and antibiotic therapy for presumptive otitis. The signs progressively worsened. The owner and the referring veterinarian noticed that the cat’s head was held low to the ground and that he put his head flat on the ground whenever he stopped moving. The cat walked around the house with this abnormal flexed position of the head, but no change was noticed in its vocalization. Examination: Video 5.68 is a series of videos showing the cat’s abnor - mal flexion of the neck and the head held closer to the ground while walking. The scapulae are positioned in a more dorsal position, pro - truding above the horizontal plane of the epaxial muscles when the cat bears weight on the limb. The thoracic limbs are “thrown forward” without an apparent ataxia. The cat was able to jump onto a chair. Not shown on the video is that whenever the cat stopped walking, he laid his head down on the ground. Evaluation of the spinal reflexes, tone of the limbs, and muscle size were all normal. The cat did not exhibit pain on manipulation of the neck, limbs, or any specific muscles. Anatomic Diagnosis: Neuromuscular disease to account for abnor - mal flexion of the neck and the protruding scapulae along with the limbs being thrown forward on protraction. Differential Diagnosis: For flexion of the neck in a cat, the list of dif - ferential diagnoses should include hypokalemia, thiamin deficiency, hyperthyroidism, myasthenia gravis, diabetes mellitus, polymyositis, and organophosphate toxicity. Stringhalt This abnormal gait is referred to as stringhalt and represents a hyper - activity of flexors of hips, stifle, tarsus, and digit aided by the recipro - cal apparatus. It involves a disorder of the central pattern generators of spinal cord segments L4 through S2, their roots, spinal nerves, spinal ganglia, femoral, sciatic, fibular, and tibial nerves. The disor - der may date back to the times of Shakespeare’s Henry VIII : “ … the spavin or springhalt among them.” Two forms are recognized: 1. The sporadic, or classic, form affects individual horses with a sudden onset, unilateral or bilateral, with no associated CNS or PNS lesions. Treatment consists of surgical removal of the tendon of insertion of the lateral digital extensor muscle or oral phenytoin. Neither of these therapies is effective in all patients. 2. The epidemic, or plant- associated, form occurs in pastured hors - es where false dandelion (flatweed, catsear, Hypochaeris radicata ) is present. This is especially common in Australia but also oc - curs in other countries, including areas in the United States. A number of weeks of grazing is required before bilateral string - halt suddenly occurs. A prolonged recovery follows immediate removal of the affected horses from that pasture. This form of stringhalt is related to a dying- back axonopathy of branches of the sciatic nerves in the pelvic limbs. Neurogenic atrophy occurs in crural muscles. Video 5.66 is an example of the epidemic form of stringhalt in a 17- year- old castrated male mixed- breed horse that developed an abnormal gait in the pelvic limbs after 3 to 4 weeks on a pasture that contained false dandelion plants. Ancillary Studies: A minimal database was collected on Bandit and included a complete blood cell count, chemistry panel, including CK levels, and serum thyroxine level. Additional tests included radiog - raphy of the thorax, abdomen, and cervical vertebral column; serum titers for acetylcholine receptor antibodies for the diagnosis of my - asthenia gravis; and serology for infectious diseases, including feline leukemia virus, feline immunodeficiency virus, Cryptococcus neofor - mans , and T. gondii . Complete blood count, thyroxine level, acetyl - choline receptor antibody test, and titers for the infectious disorders were all normal, as was radiography. The chemistry panel revealed a serum potassium concentration of 2.9 mg/dL, with normal serum potassium being 3.5 to 5.8 mg/dL. The CK was also elevated at 3900 IU/L, with normal being up to 314 IU/L. Abdominal ultrasonography revealed a right adrenal mass. Serum aldosterone levels were elevat - ed, confirming the diagnosis of an aldosterone- secreting neoplasm of the adrenal gland. CK may be elevated in hypokalemic patients. Usually, with primary myositis, the CK level is much higher. Bandit received intravenous fluids with supplemental potassium. Once his serum potassium concentration increased, his head and neck pos - ture, positioning of his scapulae, and thoracic limb gait returned to normal. The right adrenal tumor was removed. Hypokalemia has also been reported as a cause of a primary myopathy in the Burmese breed of cat and is referred to as feline hypokalemic periodic polymyopathy . In this breed, hypokalemia is associated with a defect identified in a locus on chromosome E1 and in the gene WNK4 , and a genetic test to screen for the genetic defect has been developed. These Burmese cats have a character - istic stance and gait similar to Bandit, with a flexed neck and pro - truded scapulae. Function of Cranial Nerves III, IV, and VI FUNCTION To understand the clinical signs resulting from lesions that interfere with the function of one or more of these three cranial nerves that innervate the extraocular muscles, the normal action of these muscles on the eyeball must be understood. If the eyeball is assumed to have three axes for rotation, the muscles may be grouped into three opposing pairs. If a horizontal axis through the center of the eyeball is assumed, the dorsal rectus will elevate the eyeball, and the ventral rectus will depress the eyeball. If a vertical axis through the center of the eyeball is assumed, the medial rectus will adduct the eyeball and the lateral rectus will abduct it. If an anterior- to- posterior axis through the center of the eyeball is assumed, the dorsal oblique will intort the eyeball, or rotate the dorsal portion medioventrally toward the nose, and the ventral oblique will extort the eyeball or rotate the dorsal portion lateroventrally away from the nose. This is easiest to appreciate by considering the action of the medial and lateral rectus muscles in horizontal conjugate movements. To test the action of these muscles as part of the cranial nerve examination, the veterinary patient obviously cannot be depended on to respond to the command to look in different directions. However, the function of the oculomotor nerve to the medial rectus and the abducent nerve to the lateral rectus is readily assessed by testing for normal or physiologic nystagmus, a vestibulo- ocular test. In some cooperative small animals, a food treat can be used to have the patient track it with its eyes. Nystagmus is defined as involuntary movements of the eyes; normal and abnormal forms exist. Testing for normal physiologic nystagmus is a part of the neurologic examination. Normal physiologic nystagmus is a jerk nystagmus; that is, a fast phase and a slow phase of the eye movement are present. The direction of the fast phase defines the direction of the nystagmus. The clinician stands over the small animal patient so that both are facing in the same direction; with large animals, the clinician stands facing the animal and is able to observe both eyes. The animal’s head is moved at a moderate rate from one side to the other and repeated while the clinician watches the eye movements. Alternatively, the clinician can sit on a chair that rotates, hold the patient with the patient facing them, and rotate in the chair to elicit normal nystagmus. In small animals, it may help to elevate the superior eyelid while performing this test so that the limbus can be observed. Moving the head stimulates the sensory receptors in the inner ear that are innervated by the vestibular portion of cranial nerve VIII. Impulses are relayed into the vestibular nuclei in the medulla and then passed rostrally via the medial longitudinal fasciculus to the abducent, trochlear, and oculomotor nuclei. As the animal’s head is moved to the right, both eyes will exhibit a rapid movement, a jerk toward the right. This is a normal right nystagmus. Cranial Nerve VII: Facial Neurons ANATOMY The cell bodies of the GSE neurons in the facial nerve are located in the facial nucleus in the rostral medulla. This well- defined nucleus is ventrolateral in the medulla, midway between the pyramid medially and the spinal nucleus of the trigeminal nerve laterally, caudal to the trapezoid body and the level of attachment of the caudal cerebellar peduncle to the cerebellum (see Plate 12 in Chapter 2 ). It is caudal to the dorsal nucleus of the trapezoid body and rostral to the olivary nucleus (see Fig. 6.30 ; see also Fig. 6.1 , Fig. 6.3 , and Fig. 9.12A ). The axons of these cell bodies initially pass dorsomedially to the midline of the floor of the fourth ventricle. Here they course rostrally for 1 to 2 mm dorsal to the abducent nucleus in the genu of the facial nerve. Then they course ventrolaterally through the medulla, medial to the spinal nucleus and tract of the trigeminal nerve and lateral to the dorsal nucleus of the trapezoid body. They emerge through the lateral aspect of the trapezoid body ventral to the vestibulocochlear nerve (see Plate 11 in Chapter 2 ). The facial nerve accompanies the vestibulocochlear nerve into the internal acoustic meatus of the petrosal portion of the temporal bone. 3 The facial nerve is more dorsal; it enters the facial canal within the temporal bone and emerges through the stylomastoid foramen. Branches of the facial nerve are distributed to the muscles of facial expression. These are the relatively thin muscles of the ear, eyelids, nose, cheeks, and lips. The facial nerve also innervates the caudal portion of the digastricus muscle, which belongs to the muscles of mastication ( Fig. 6.31 ). CLINICAL SIGNS Lesions of the facial nucleus or the facial nerve from the medulla to where it emerges from the stylomastoid foramen result in complete facial paresis or in paralysis and inability to move these facial muscles normally. Paralysis may be recognized by asymmetry of the face, but that depends on the species. The most obvious asymmetry occurs in the horse ( Fig. 6.32 ); on the paralyzed side, the ear droops, the palpebral fissure is decreased in size, the superior lip droops, and the nose and superior lip are markedly deviated toward the normal side because of the unopposed contractions of the muscles of the lip and nose on that side. As evidence of a drooping of the superior eyelid, examine the angle of the eyelid lashes. Even the angle of the superior eyelid lashes is decreased on the affected side when the two sides are carefully compared. The ear droop, small palpebral fissure, deviated nose, and lip droop are usually seen in small ruminants, except for the ear in breeds with pendular ears, for example, the Nubian goat. The clinical signs show continual contraction of the muscles innervated by the facial nerve on one side. The facial nerve function on the opposite side is usually normal. These patients present with a slight elevation of the ear, a narrowed palpebral fissure, taut lips with the angle (corner of the lips) drawn caudally, and a nose slightly deviated toward the affected side ( Figs. 6.51 and 6.52 ). Some affected patients exhibit slight movement of the eyelids, lips, and ear when those areas are stimulated. This syndrome has been called hemifacial spasm , but that is a misnomer because it is not a spasm of the muscles in the true sense. The neuromuscular activity is constant. Some clinicians have described it to be the result of complete facial paralysis with denervation atrophy that causes shortening (contracture) of the facial muscles. We disagree with this hypothesis for the following reason: If general anesthesia is administered to one of these patients or if the affected buccal branches are blocked by a local anesthetic, the clinical signs immediately disappear; they return when the anesthetic has resolved. This supports the idea that a neurogenic stimulus is the basis for these clinical signs rather than a denervation atrophy or other myopathic disorder. A more accurate name for this disorder is facial tetanus . As a clinical sign, tetanus is defined as the sustained contraction of muscles without relaxation; it is caused by high- frequency repetitive stimulation of the motor neurons innervating the muscles involved. Some of these patients have otitis media on the affected side, with or without signs of dysfunction in the vestibular system or in the sympathetic innervation of the head. However, a significant number have no clinical signs of otitis media, and MRI and CT are normal. We have performed MRIs on numerous affected dogs and cats in which the combined facial and vestibulocochlear nerves were visible on both sides of the medulla and no lesions were recognized; no evidence of otitis media was observed. These tetanic signs may be the first indication of a disorder of the facial nerve or may follow a facial paralysis. The tetanic signs do not respond well to antibiotic therapy for otitis media when it is present. It appears that facial tetanus is permanent in most patients. The neurophysiologic mechanism of facial tetanus is unknown. If it were a central functional disinhibition at the level of the facial nucleus, we would expect it to be bilateral. Until we have a better explanation, we assume facial tetanus is the result of an irritative lesion, such as a neuritis affecting the LMN within the extraparenchymal facial nerve. Idiopathic Facial Neuropathy: Neuritis Idiopathic facial paralysis occurs in older dogs; the cocker spaniel and beagle are at some risk for this disorder, but it has been seen in many breeds. Occasionally, an affected dog has hypothyroidism, but its relationship to the facial nerve disorder is still speculative. 1 , 2 Clinical laboratory studies should support hypothyroidism such as severe hypercholesterolemia. 2 An ischemic neuropathy may be the result of hypothyroid- induced atherosclerosis or increased blood viscosity. In some dogs the facial paralysis is accompanied by clinical signs of involvement of the vestibular portion of cranial nerve VIII. 46 Response of these dogs to thyroid supplementation is usually satisfactory. Ultimately this idiopathic disorder is a diagnosis of exclusion that is based on the inability to diagnose any causative abnormality using routine MRI or CT, evaluation of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), and metabolic studies. An MRI study of the intratemporal course of the facial nerve in patients with this clinical diagnosis showed contrast enhancement in at least one segment of the facial nerve in three of the five dogs that were studied. 43 Further studies are needed to correlate this information with morphologic change in the affected facial nerve segment. It is not known whether any neuritis is involved. Bell palsy in humans is a similar facial paralysis and is believed to be related to infection by the herpes simplex virus. The accessory nerve also has a spinal cord component. These medullary cell bodies are topographically organized in the nucleus ambiguus, with the most rostral portion contributing to the glossopharyngeal nerve and the most caudal to the accessory nerve. Nucleus ambiguus is an ill- defined column of neuronal cell bodies located ventrolaterally in the medulla, medial to the spinal tract and nucleus of the trigeminal nerve (see Plate 15 in Chapter 2 ). It extends from the facial nucleus rostrally through the caudal medulla to a level slightly caudal to the obex. It is continued by the motor nucleus of the accessory nerve through the gray matter of all the cervical spinal cord segments ( Fig. 6.53 ). These two sets of axons form the motor portion of the glossopharyngeal nerve just caudal to the vestibulocochlear nerve. The glossopharyngeal nerve courses through the jugular foramen and emerges from the skull via the tympano- occipital fissure, where it contributes pharyngeal branches to innervate the stylopharyngeus muscle and other pharyngeal muscles via the pharyngoesophageal plexus, which includes a contribution from the vagus nerve. They are joined by the GVE preganglionic axons from the parasympathetic nucleus of the vagus to form the motor portion of the vagus nerve. CLINICAL SIGNS Lesions in the motor nucleus of the accessory nerve, in the GSE axons of the internal branch of the accessory nerve, in the cervical portion of the vagus nerve or its recurrent laryngeal branch, or in the caudal laryngeal nerve or the muscles of the larynx result in laryngeal paralysis. This causes an inspiratory dyspnea. When this is recognized in most small animals, bilateral paralysis is considered to exist. In racehorses, unilateral laryngeal paralysis is a common cause of poor performance; the inspiratory dyspnea may be severe enough to be audible in the exercised horse and is referred to as roaring . The inspiratory dyspnea is caused by the failure of the cricoarytenoideus dorsalis muscles to completely abduct the vocal folds, which interferes with airflow on inspiration. Lesions in the nucleus ambiguus, in the GSE axons in the vagus and glossopharyngeal nerves, or in the muscles of the pharynx or esophagus result in varying degrees of difficulty in swallowing, or dysphagia . In unilateral pharyngeal paresis, partial ability to swallow exists, but choking, gagging, and loss of food through the nares occur. With bilateral lesions, swallowing cannot be accomplished, choking and gagging are worse, and food commonly appears at the nares. With complete lesions, no gag reflex is present in small animals, but with partial lesions, this is a difficult reflex to assess because of the marked variation in normal animals. In some dog breeds, especially the Labrador and the golden retriever, this reflex is difficult to assess reliably. The most reliable indication of a problem with swallowing is the owner’s description of the patient’s attempts to eat and drink or making gagging noises when eating. We have not observed in any domestic animal reliable clinical signs of lesions involving portions of the GSE neurons in the accessory nerve that innervate the cervical muscles. A congenital idiopathic megaesophagus causes regurgitation that is first observed as the puppy is weaned from a liquid to a solid- food diet. A late- onset acquired form of megaesophagus occurs in older dogs and in some dogs is believed to be a form of focal myasthenia gravis. (This condition is described with the spinal nerve LMN diseases in Chapter 5 . ) Congenital idiopathic megaesophagus has been reported in most breeds of dogs, but a high incidence is seen in the German shepherd dog, great dane, Irish setter, Labrador retriever, Chinese Shar- Pei, and Newfoundland breeds. Canine Laryngeal Paralysis Canine laryngeal paralysis occurs as an inherited disorder or an acquired disease. Inherited . Laryngeal paralysis in dogs is most commonly an inherited degenerative disorder of the neurons in the recurrent laryngeal nerves. The neuropathy may affect only the recurrent laryngeal nerves, or their dysfunction may be part of a more dif - fuse polyneuropathy or may be a combination of neuropathy and encephalomyelopathy. It is critical to discuss with owners the possibility of a generalized neuromuscular disorder or encepha - lomyelopathy before any surgical correction of the larynx. In our opinion the most thorough study of inherited laryngeal paralysis was performed at the University of Utrecht in young bouvier des Flandres dogs. 44 , 45 These young dogs were presented at 4 to 6 months of age for not growing well, exhibiting fatigue to the point of collapse and even cyanosis when exercised, and noisy breathing caused by the marked inspiratory dyspnea. These severe clinical signs of laryngeal paralysis required that laryngeal surgery be performed if the patients were to survive. Studies determined that this progressive laryngeal paresis was caused by bilateral abiotrophy of neurons in the motor nucleus of the accessory nerve within the nucleus ambiguous that innervated the larynx, and this abiotrophy was inherited as an autosomal dominant genetic disorder. Responsible, dedicated breeders in the Netherlands were able to eliminate this disorder from the breed in a few years of careful control of their selection of dogs for breeding. North American dog breeders could take some lessons from these responsible individuals. A similar progressive laryngeal paresis- to- paralysis is inherited in young Siberian husky and husky cross- bred racing dogs, bull terriers, and Dalmatians. Dalmatians may also have other signs of polyneuropathy. The patterns of inheritance and the pathologies of these disorders are less well defined than are those of the Bouviers. Chapter 5 describes laryngeal paralysis in 1- to 3- year- old Leonbergers that is followed in a few weeks by the clinical signs of progressive sciatic- tibial nerve deficit. 39 In time, tetraparesis occurs. It is inherited as an autosomal recessive genetic disorder. Take, for example, an 8- to 10- week- old Rottweiler pup that is presented with the clinical signs of progressive laryngeal paresis. Inspiratory dyspnea may be the only clinical sign that is recognized, and surgery is performed to establish an adequate airway. At 12 to 14 weeks of age, the owner comes back with this dog because of a progressive gait abnormality that can be recognized as a dysfunction of the UMN and general proprioceptive tracts in the spinal cord. This is an inherited autosomal genetic disorder that affects the central nervous system (CNS), especially the spinal cord tracts and the peripheral nervous system, especially the recurrent laryngeal nerves. The initial clinical signs may involve the pelvic limb gait, the larynx, or both. If the clinical signs of laryngeal paresis occur first, surgery may be performed on a dog to alleviate signs of laryngeal paralysis. In a few weeks to months, the pup will exhibit the signs of a progressive gait disorder that will ultimately cause recumbency and warrant euthanasia. This inherited disease of Rottweiler dogs was published in 1997 as a neuronal vacuolation and spinocerebellar degeneration, but in reality, this is a diffuse encephalomyelopathy and polyneuropathy. 25 This disorder is described in Case 10.16, in the discussion on small animal cervical spinal cord diseases. Progressive laryngeal paresis has also been reported in Dalmatian dogs as part of a diffuse polyneuropathy that causes LMN tetraparesis. 26 We are not aware of a progressive laryngeal paresis in the Rottweiler breed that is not associated with other neurologic signs and lesions, so great care should be taken before performing laryngeal surgical intervention. Feline Laryngeal Paralysis Laryngeal paralysis is uncommon in cats. 37 Unilateral paresis may follow injury to the carotid sheath during venipuncture, dog bites, or thyroid surgery. An infiltrative lymphoma may cause unilateral or bilateral laryngeal paresis. It is likely that a unilateral paralysis will not be recognized clinically because of the relative inactivity of this species. We have treated two adult cats with severe otitis media- interna that presented with concurrent signs of laryngeal paralysis. On MRI of the head in both cats, there was abnormal enhancement of the tissues external to the tympanic bullae that extended caudomedially to affect the vagus exiting the tympano- occipital fissure. Injury Be aware that laryngeal hemiparesis or hemiplegia may follow an injury to the recurrent laryngeal nerve, where it is still a component of the vagus nerve in the carotid sheath in the neck. At this site it is at risk for injury by aggressive attempts at external jugular venipuncture in any species, dog bites in small animals, ventral surgical approaches to the cervical intervertebral discs in dogs, and thyroid surgery. Cranial Nerve XII: Hypoglossal Neurons ANATOMY The GSE neuronal cell bodies are located in the motor nucleus of the hypoglossal nerve in the medulla (see Plates 14 and 15 in Chapter 2 ). 6 This nucleus is adjacent to the median plane and to the floor of the fourth ventricle. It is a long nucleus (3 to 5 mm), extending from the obex caudally nearly to the level of the acoustic stria rostrally (see Figs. 6.1 and 6.3 ). The axons pass directly ventrally and slightly laterally through the reticular formation and across the lateral portion of the olivary nucleus. They emerge lateral to the pyramid as a longitudinal series of small roots. This row of hypoglossal roots merges at the small hypoglossal canal to form the hypoglossal nerve. The neurons course to innervate the extrinsic tongue muscles (styloglossus, hyoglossus, and genioglossus), the intrinsic tongue muscles, and the geniohyoideus. The hypoglossal nerve is much smaller inside the cranial cavity than outside the skull. This is the result of the increase in myelination and connective tissue that occurs after the nerve emerges from the hypoglossal canal. CLINICAL SIGNS Lesions in any parts of these hypoglossal neurons result in impairment of the function of the tongue in prehension, deglutition, mastication, and phonation. The most reliable clinical sign of a unilateral lesion is atrophy of the tongue. The clinician must be sure to look at the tongue carefully during the cranial nerve examination because it is easy to miss mild unilateral atrophy. If the patient protrudes its tongue, it may deviate toward the denervated side because of contraction of the normal contralateral genioglossus and intrinsic tongue muscles. The animal may be observed to lick its lips only on the paralyzed side. An early sign of a unilateral hypoglossal nerve lesion may be the presence of fasciculations of the denervated half of the tongue, before atrophy is apparent. DISEASES In the dog, unilateral tongue atrophy is most commonly caused by an extraparenchymal lesion involving the hypoglossal nerve roots ( Fig. 6.62 ). In the horse, unilateral tongue atrophy is almost pathognomonic for an S. neurona infection of the hypoglossal nucleus in the medulla (see Case 6.2 ). This may occur with or without other signs of medullary disease. In some horses the lesion is limited to this nucleus, and at autopsy the cell bodies are seen to have been replaced by astrocytes. In cattle the most common cause of tongue paresis is encephalitis in the medulla caused by L. monocytogenes ( Fig. 6.63 ). The clinician must be sure to pull the tongue out of the mouth in horses and cattle to assess tongue strength and to look for atrophy. This is much more difficult to do in normal cattle than in horses. Care must be taken not to be too aggressive because it is possible to injure the hypoglossal innervation and have the tongue fail to be retracted into the mouth. A hyoid bone fracture or careless stylohyoid bone surgery in horses may also injure the hypoglossal nerve on one side. Clinical signs of vestibular nerve dysfunction associated with otitis media- interna are common in domestic animals. However, to avoid overlooking a central cause of vestibular system signs, it is important to look carefully for involvement of other closely associated cranial nerves such as the hypoglossal nerve.

203 General Anatomy General visceral efferent (GVE) neurons innervate the smooth muscle associated with blood vessels and visceral structures, glands, and cardiac muscle. This is an involuntary system that represents the lower motor neuron (LMN) for the autonomic nervous system. The autonomic nervous system is an anatomic and physiologic system with central and peripheral components. It includes higher centers located in the hypothalamus, midbrain, pons, and medulla. The hypothalamus is the primary integrating center of the autonomic nervous system. In general, nuclei in its rostral portion subserve the parasympathetic division of the GVE- LMN. Nuclei in its caudal portion subserve the sympathetic division of the GVE- LMN. These hypothalamic nuclei receive afferents from the cerebrum by way of numerous pathways, from thalamic nuclei, and from ascending general visceral afferent (GVA) pathways. The hypothalamus influences the activity of the metabolic centers in the reticular formation of the midbrain, pons, and medulla. These centers control the activity of visceral smooth muscle, glands, and cardiac muscle by means of the GVE- LMN, which is located in specific cranial nerves and all spinal nerves. Some texts consider that the autonomic nervous system is only the GVE- LMN, but this is too simplistic and not realistic. The autonomic nervous system is concerned with activating emergency mechanisms and with the repair and preservation of the internal environment of the body. It maintains a steady state in the internal environment for the continuous efficient function of the body, which is called homeostasis . In order to carry out these functions, the autonomic nervous system must receive information from the body via sensory neurons in the GVA system, then process this information in centers in the brain, and finally send responses back to the body by brainstem and spinal cord pathways that activate LMNs of the GVE system. The GVE system is grouped physiologically and anatomically into two components: the sympathetic and parasympathetic systems. This LMN system is a two- neuron system connecting the central nervous system (CNS) to the effector organ innervated. These two neurons are referred to as preganglionic and postganglionic neurons (i.e., distal to the ganglion). However, to be accurate, only the axon of the second neuron in this LMN system is postganglionic, as its cell body is in the ganglion. In this text, the second neuron in this two- neuron LMN pathway is referred to as the ganglionic neuron . The axon of this second neuron is called the ganglionic or postganglionic axon . The cell body of the preganglionic neuron is in the gray matter of the CNS, and the cell body of the ganglionic neuron is in a ganglion in the peripheral nervous system. The telodendron of the preganglionic neuron synapses on the dendritic zone of the cell body of the ganglionic neuron in this ganglion. The sympathetic system is referred to as the thoracolumbar system , based on the location of the cell body of the preganglionic neuron (first neuron), which is in the lateral gray column from spinal cord segments T1 to about L4 or L5. As a general rule, the sympathetic ganglia are located fairly close to the CNS, and the ganglionic axons are fairly long. With a few exceptions, the neurotransmitter released at the telodendron of the sympathetic ganglionic axon is norepinephrine. Thus, the sympathetic system is referred to as the adrenergic system . The parasympathetic system is referred to as the craniosacral system because the cell bodies of the preganglionic neurons are located either in the brainstem nuclei of cranial nerves III, VII, IX, and X or in the sacral spinal cord segments. For clinical purposes, the GVE- LMN may be divided into four components: (1) control of the pupils, (2) control of micturition and defecation, (3) control of the enteric system, and (4) control of the cardiorespiratory system. Veterinary students and clinicians must understand the functional anatomy of all four of these components. Control of the Pupils For the purposes of clinical neurology, the size of the pupils can be thought of as representing a constant balance between the amount of light in the environment and the emotional status of the patient. The parasympathetic GVE system regulates the response of the eye to environmental light. The sympathetic GVE system regulates the response of the pupil to environmental factors that elicit stress, such as excitement, fear, and anger. PARASYMPATHETIC INNERVATION OF THE EYE A standard part of the cranial nerve evaluation in the neurologic examination of any patient includes assessment of the size and symmetry of the pupils and their response to a strong source of light. The use of a strong penlight is crucial for an accurate assessment of the parasympathetic innervation to the eye. Always evaluate the size of the resting pupils first, and do it from a distance, with just enough light to see the pupils. In some animals, the pupils are difficult to see in ambient light. The examination may be facilitated by holding a light source on the median plane at about the level of the end of the patient’s nose or a few inches beyond in cats and brachycephalic dog breeds— just far enough away so that you do not elicit significant pupil constriction. Appreciate the size of the resting pupils and look for any asymmetry, called anisocoria . Remember that an overly excited or scared patient may have equally dilated pupils as the result of circulating epinephrine. Place your light source up close to the eye without touching it. Record the response and swing the light quickly to the other eye. Record that response and swing the light back to the first eye. Repeat this several times to be sure of your evaluation. This requires patience, both from you and from the animal being evaluated. Usually a normal, excited patient with dilated pupils will respond to a bright light source. To appreciate the pupillary responses or lack thereof, all the anatomic components involved in the test and all the factors that control the size of the pupils must be understood. These components and factors are described below. Anatomy The sensory anatomic pathway stimulated by the light is described in detail in Chapter 14 . In brief ( Fig. 7.1 ), the light must initially stimulate receptors in the retina. The area centralis of the retina is the most sensitive to light and in carnivores is just lateral to the optic disc. The impulses generated in the retina travel through the optic nerve of that eye and continue in both optic tracts to the most dorsal caudal and lateral aspects of the metathalamus, where they pass over the lateral geniculate nuclei and enter the pretectal nuclei in the most rostral part of the midbrain (see Fig. 2.18A). Synapse occurs there, and most of the axons of the pretectal neurons cross through the caudal commissure to synapse on the GVE parasympathetic LMNs in the contralateral parasympathetic nucleus of the oculomotor nerve in the midbrain (see Plate 5 in Chapter 2 ). A smaller number of pretectal neurons project directly to the ipsilateral parasympathetic nucleus of the oculomotor nerve. Thus, light directed into one eye stimulates a series of neurons that terminate in both parasympathetic nuclei of the oculomotor nerve, resulting in a response in both eyes. The response observed in the eye that is stimulated is the direct response. The simultaneous response in the contralateral eye is the indirect or consensual response. For this GVE- LMN pathway, the cell bodies of the preganglionic neurons are located in the parasympathetic nucleus of the oculomotor nerve in the rostral part of the mesencephalon. In humans, this nucleus is known as the nucleus of Edinger- Westphal , though we discourage the use of this eponym. The parasympathetic nucleus of the oculomotor nerve GVE is located in the ventral part of the central gray substance next to the median plane, where it is continuous caudally with the general somatic efferent (GSE) motor nucleus of the oculomotor nerve that is described in Chapter 6 . The GVE axons course ventrally and emerge with the GSE axons in the lateral aspect of the interpeduncular fossa on the medial side of the crus cerebri. The GVE axons in the canine oculomotor nerve are located superficially on the medial side of this cranial nerve, where they are at risk for being compressed by extraparenchymal mass like a neoplasm and by disorders that cause the mesencephalon to swell or be displaced. The oculomotor nerve, with the GVE preganglionic axons, passes through the orbital fissure into the orbit within the periorbita, where it is adjacent to the lateral side of the optic nerve for a few millimeters. The oculomotor nerve then abruptly branches to innervate the extraocular muscles (dorsal, ventral, and medial recti muscles and the ventral oblique muscle). Located at this point of branching is a small ganglion, the ciliary ganglion. In this ganglion, the telodendrons of the GVE preganglionic neurons synapse on the dendritic zones of the cell bodies of the ganglionic neurons. The ganglionic axons pass by way of short ciliary nerves along the surface of the optic nerve to the eyeball to innervate the smooth muscle of the ciliary muscle and the sphincter of the pupil. 13 The more light that enters the eyes and stimulates the retina, the more impulses will reach this parasympathetic pathway and cause the sphincter muscles to contract, which constricts the pupils. In diminished light, stimulus to these sphincter muscles is less, so the pupils passively dilate. The lights in the room need to be turned off to observe this. Directing light into one eye evaluates the direct response in that eye and the indirect or consensual response in the opposite eye. This consensual response is appreciated when swinging the light to the opposite eye. This terminology can be very confusing, and it is much better to record what happens in each eye when swinging the light back and forth. Sometimes, the degree of constriction seen in the eye in which the light is directed (the direct response) is slightly greater than the degree of constriction seen in the opposite eye (indirect, consensual response). This is normal. It occurs because most of the axons in the optic nerve cross at the optic chiasm. After synapsing in the pretectal nucleus, most of the axons projecting from the pretectal neurons cross back via the caudal commissure. As a

Pharmacologic testing involves exposing the eye to direct- acting and indirect- acting parasympathomimetic drugs applied topically into the eye. The direct- acting drugs, such as pilocarpine, elicit a more rapid and more complete response in the hypersensitive denervated muscle (ganglionic lesion), whereas the indirect- acting drugs require the ganglionic neurons to be intact and able to release their endogenous neurotransmitter at the sphincter muscle in the iris. Imaging procedures are available, and we strongly recommend their use in locating lesions in this pupillary light reflex pathway. In large specialty practices and teaching hospitals, the most common cause of dilated pupils in an otherwise normal hospitalized patient is the mydriatic drug used to dilate the pupils for the ophthalmologic examination of the fundus of the eyes. The same is true for the unfortunate veterinary student, intern, or technician who touches his or her eye after using these medications. Always check to see that topical medications have not been used before examining the patient. SYMPATHETIC INNERVATION OF THE EYE Anatomy The preganglionic neuronal cell bodies are located in the lateral gray horn of the first three or four thoracic spinal cord segments (see Fig. 7.1 ). The axons pass through the ventral gray horn and adjacent white matter to join the ventral roots of these segments and the proximal portion of the segmental spinal nerve. Just beyond where the spinal nerve emerges from the intervertebral foramen and before it branches, these preganglionic neuronal axons leave the spinal nerve in the segmental ramus communicans, which joins the thoracic sympathetic trunk. The latter courses longitudinally inside the thorax along the ventrolateral aspect of the vertebral bodies. The preganglionic neuronal axons destined for the head usually do not synapse in a trunk ganglion but continue cranially in the trunk, pass through the cervicothoracic and middle cervical ganglia, and course cranially in the neck in the cervical sympathetic trunk. This trunk is associated with the vagus nerve forming the vagosympathetic trunk, which is located in the carotid sheath (see Fig. 7.26 ). At the level of the head, medial to the origin of the digastricus muscle and ventromedial to the tympanic bulla, the cervical sympathetic trunk separates from the vagus nerve and terminates in the cranial cervical ganglion. Here, the preganglionic neuronal telodendria synapse on the dendritic zones of the ganglionic cell bodies. The pathway from this ganglion to the ocular structures is not well defined. 1 , 3 , 47 , 54 As a rule these ganglionic sympathetic neuronal axons follow the blood vessels to the effector organs. A 1932 description of this pathway in the cat showed these axons to pass through the tympanic cavity on the ventral surface of the petrous portion of the temporal bone, but it is not clear whether these axons are truly sympathetic ganglionic axons or rather the parasympathetic preganglionic axons of the glossopharyngeal nerve that form the tympanic plexus and are known to course in this position. 47 It is also possible that the sympathetic ganglionic axons may join the tympanic plexus with the parasympathetic preganglionic axons. From this location in the tympanic cavity, these axons enter the cranial cavity ventral to the trigeminal ganglion and join the ophthalmic nerve branch of the trigeminal nerve, which enters the orbit within the periorbita. Here, these sympathetic axons innervate the orbitalis muscle, which is the smooth muscle in the periorbita and the eyelids, including the third eyelid, and the smooth muscle that makes up the ciliaris muscle, and the smooth muscle of the dilator pupillae in the iris ( Fig. 7.7 ). This pathway correlates well with our clinical experience of recognizing a paralysis of these muscles (Horner syndrome) in the presence of otitis media in small animals and trigeminal neuritis in dogs. Another possible route from the cranial cervical ganglion to the ophthalmic nerve is in the internal carotid nerve, which leaves the cranial cervical ganglion and follows the internal carotid artery as a plexus of axons. This artery lies adjacent to this ganglion, where these ganglionic neuronal axons may join it. The artery then passes through the tympano- occipital fissure and enters the carotid canal. Lesions involving the tympano- occipital fissure can result in Horner syndrome in the eye ipsilateral to the lesion. The carotid canal is formed in the dorsomedial aspect of the tympanic bulla adjacent and lateral to the petro- occipital canal. At the rostral end of the carotid canal, the artery and these axons gain access to the cranial cavity through the foramen lacerum, which is located just ventral to where the trigeminal nerve emerges from the rostral aspect of the canal for the trigeminal nerve in the petrous portion of the temporal bone. At this point, postganglionic sympathetic axons may join the ophthalmic nerve. Some axons may continue along the internal carotid artery to the cerebral arterial circle and leave the circle with the internal ophthalmic artery, which courses along the optic nerves through the optic canals to enter the orbit within the periorbita. This last pathway has recently been described in humans. We have also seen microscopically numerous unmyelinated axons coursing with the internal carotid artery in the carotid canal. As you see by the preceding description, the exact route of the axons from this cranial cervical ganglion to the smooth muscle of the ocular structures remains to be better defined. However, it turns out there is an anatomic site where infections in the tympanic cavity (middle ear) of small animals may readily affect these axons. Otitis media in horses and farm animals does not, however, affect these sympathetic neurons.
